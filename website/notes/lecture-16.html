1. Lecture Overview

    2. The business environment.
    3. How operational systems differ from informational systems.
    4. Business intelligence.
    5. The architecture and main components of a data warehouse.

  • Also on the slides (second half): dimensional modelling, the star schema, information packages, the data cube, hierarchies (drill-down/roll-up), STARjoin/STARindex/materialised views, and the SQL used for data warehousing.

Example (added for clarity)

Lecture 22 (data mining) said mining can run on data "stored in a warehouse." This lecture explains what that warehouse is, how it differs from the database that runs the business day to day, and how it is structured for analysis (star schema, cubes, roll-up/drill-down).

2. The Business Environment

Question Answer (from the slides)
What is the purpose of a business? To make money
What do businesses sell? Products or services
To whom do they sell? Customers (individuals or organisations)
From whom do they get what they sell? Suppliers or internal
How are operations organised? Business units, business processes
Who makes decisions? Managers
Major threats? Losing customers, closing down
  • Why it matters: managers need timely, accurate information to make good decisions and avoid those threats — which is the job of informational systems.

3. Operational vs Informational Systems

3.1 Operational Systems

  • Purpose: "make the wheels of the business turn" — record and process business transactions as they happen.
  • Examples: point-of-sale (POS) system, inventory management system, financial systems (e.g., general ledger), insurance claims processing.
  • Used by: shop-floor workers (who interact directly with customers).
  • Classified as: OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) systems.

3.2 Informational Systems

  • Purpose: "watching the wheels of the business turn" — support informed decision-making.
  • Example usage: "Show me the top-selling products"; "Show me the problem branches"; "Alert me when a branch sells below target."
  • Used by: managers (and business analysts).
  • Classified as: OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) systems.

3.3 Comparison (consolidated, added for clarity)

Aspect Operational (OLTP) Informational (OLAP)
Metaphor Make the wheels turn Watch the wheels turn
Purpose Record & process transactions as they happen Support informed decision-making
Users Shop-floor workers, clerks Managers, business analysts
Typical request "Record this sale" "Which products sold best this quarter?"
Data Current, detailed, application-specific Current + historical, integrated, designed for analysis
Examples POS, inventory, general ledger, claims Dashboards, reports, data warehouse

Example (added)

At a supermarket, the till (POS) that scans your items and updates stock is OLTP. The head-office report "Show the five branches furthest below monthly sales target" is OLAP — it reads across many transactions, branches and months. Running that big report on the live till database would slow the tills down — one reason a separate warehouse exists.

Link to Lecture 20: OLTP (short, frequent, concurrent transactions) is exactly the workload NewSQL targets; OLAP is the analytical counterpart.

3.4 Dashboards (slide: "Tools: Dashboards — day-to-day management")

  • Analogy on the slide: managing a business is like driving a car or flying a large aircraft — you need instruments (dashboards) showing the key indicators at a glance.
  • The slide's screenshot shows a BI dashboard: a trend line of "net promoter score" by month for a selected city and a set of gauges per product line — i.e., key indicators, trends and comparisons on one screen.

4. Business Intelligence (BI)

  • BI refers to a class of applications and technologies used to:
    • gather data,
    • provide access to it,
    • and analyse data about business operations.
  • BI systems give businesses in-depth knowledge of the factors that affect the business, e.g.:
    • Customer and supplier behaviour.
    • Sales volumes and production volumes.
    • Other internal operations.
  • Result: this knowledge helps managers make better business decisions.

5. Why Data Warehousing Is a Viable Solution for Informational Systems

The slide gives three reasons:

  1. Data from multiple applications — the warehouse integrates data from POS, inventory, etc.
  2. Content includes current and historical data.
  3. Data designed for analytical tasks — organised around key business measures and business dimensions (Section 9).

Example (added)

The POS system knows today's sales; the inventory system knows stock; the finance system knows costs. A warehouse loads all three, keeps years of history, and organises them so a manager can ask "revenue by product, branch and month" in one query.

6. The Data Warehouse — Definition

A data warehouse is a collection of data to support management decisions that is:

Property Meaning (from the slides) Example
Subject-oriented Data is organised along business subjects Sales, policies, claims
Integrated Data extracted from different operational systems Claims processing + policy sales
Non-volatile Generally read-oriented, but data can be updated through controlled loading and correction processes Loaded in batches; not changed by everyday transactions
Time-variant Time is explicitly stored with the data → supports analysis along the time dimension Sales per day, week, month, quarter, year

Note: the slide's wording for non-volatile is more careful than the classic "data is never updated": warehouses are mostly read-only for users, with controlled loads and corrections.

9. Dimensional Modelling

  • Warehouse data is organised by subject, business dimensions and measures (facts).

9.1 Examples of Business Dimensions (from the slides)

Business Subject Dimensions
Supermarket Sales Time, branch, product, promotion
Airline company Frequent flyers Time, customer, flight, fare class, airport
Hotel chain Hotel occupancy Time, hotel branch, room type
  • Key point: dimensions and measures differ between businesses, but the TIME dimension is very common because historical, time-based analysis matters in many businesses. However, not every dimensional model requires a time dimension.

9.2 Information From a Data Warehouse — the Kind of Question Asked

Marketing VP of a hotel chain: "How much revenue did luxury rooms generate, month by month, in the Gauteng province, by hotel branch?"

Analysis of the question (added):

Part of question Element
"How much revenue" Measure (fact)
"luxury rooms" Room type dimension (filter)
"month by month" Time dimension (level: month)
"Gauteng province" Hotel dimension (filter at province level)
"by hotel branch" Hotel dimension (level: branch)
  • Users analyse performance by business dimensions and performance measures. → Hence the need for a database structure that supports dimensional analysis — the star schema.

9.3 Information Packages

  • Created as business requirements are gathered; one information package per specific subject.

Worked Example — Slide: information package for the subject Hotel occupancy

TIME HOTEL ROOM TYPE
Year Hotel line Room type
Quarter Province Size
Month Branch etc.

Facts (key measures): occupied rooms, vacant rooms, revenue.

Step-by-step reading:

  1. Columns = dimensions (Time, Hotel, Room type).
  2. Entries under each dimension = its hierarchy/categories, listed from the highest level to the lowest (Time: Year → Quarter → Month; Hotel: Hotel line → Province → Branch).
  3. Facts at the bottom = the numeric measures to be analysed.
  4. This one table captures the business requirements and later maps directly to the dimension tables and fact table (Section 11).

10. The Data Mart

  • The lecture overview lists "the data mart and the main reasons for implementing a data mart." On the slides, the data mart appears in the architecture figure (component 5), as the star schema location ("The Star Schema (for data mart)"), and in the roles figure — but the reasons are not listed on a slide.
  • Definition (added): a data mart is a smaller, focused subset of the data warehouse serving one department, business unit or subject (e.g., a Sales mart, a Finance mart).

10.1 Typical Reasons for Implementing a Data Mart (added — check against the textbook, Chapter 29)

  1. Focus: serves one department's specific needs (simpler, more relevant).
  2. Performance: smaller data volume → faster queries for that group.
  3. Faster, cheaper, lower-risk to build than an enterprise-wide warehouse; results arrive sooner.
  4. Easier to use: users see only the subject-specific dimensions and measures they need.
  5. Access control/security: a department sees only its own data.
  6. Ownership: a department can manage its own mart.

11. The Star Schema (for the data mart) — ROLAP

  • The star schema provides Relational OLAP (ROLAP) — a dimensional view of data using relational structures and queries.
  • Structure:
    • A central fact table (e.g., Sales).
    • Surrounding dimension tables (e.g., Time, Branch, Promotion, Product).
    • Links are formed by foreign keys in the fact table; each link is a one-to-many relationship (one dimension row → many fact rows).
  • Fact table row: represents a business event or observation at a defined level of granularity; it contains foreign keys referencing the dimension tables and one or more measures.
                 TIME
                  │
   BRANCH ─── SALES (fact) ─── PROMOTION
                  │
               PRODUCT

11.1 Worked Example — Creating the Hotel Star Schema (from the slides)

Dimension tables and fact table derived from the information package:

OCCUPANCY_FACTS                    HOTEL                 RM_TYPE              TIME
---------------                    -----                 -------              ----
Time_Key   (FK)  ───────────────►  Hotel_Key (PK)        Room_Key (PK)        Time_Key (PK)
Hotel_Key  (FK)                    Hotel_code            Room_code            Time_code
Room_Key   (FK)                    Hotel_Line            Room_Type            Year
Occupied_rooms                     Province              Size                 Quarter
Vacant_rooms                       Branch                                     Month
Revenue

Step-by-step reading:

  1. HOTEL dimension holds the Hotel_Line → Province → Branch hierarchy; RM_TYPE holds Room_Type and Size; TIME holds Year → Quarter → Month.
  2. Each dimension table has its own surrogate key (Hotel_Key, Room_Key, Time_Key) and a code (Hotel_code, …) from the source systems.
  3. OCCUPANCY_FACTS holds three foreign keys (one per dimension) + three measures (Occupied_rooms, Vacant_rooms, Revenue).
  4. Granularity: one fact row = occupancy and revenue for one hotel branch, one room type, one time period (the lowest level in each dimension).
  5. Mapping rule: information package → dimension tables (columns of the package) + fact table (the facts row).
  6. Hierarchy levels are denormalised into one wide dimension table (e.g., Province and Branch in the same HOTEL table) — typical of a star schema; it reduces joins (the more normalised version is a "snowflake" — added, not on the slides).

11.2 Answering a Question with SQL (added — the slide says "SQL statements are executed to provide answers to users' questions")

Question: How much revenue was realised from luxury rooms by province by month?

SELECT   h.Province, t.Year, t.Month, SUM(f.Revenue) AS total_revenue
FROM     OCCUPANCY_FACTS f
JOIN     HOTEL   h ON f.Hotel_Key = h.Hotel_Key
JOIN     RM_TYPE r ON f.Room_Key  = r.Room_Key
JOIN     TIME    t ON f.Time_Key  = t.Time_Key
WHERE    r.Room_Type = 'Luxury'
GROUP BY h.Province, t.Year, t.Month;

Step-by-step walkthrough:

  1. FROM OCCUPANCY_FACTS f — start at the fact table (the centre of the star).
  2. Three JOINs — link the fact table to each dimension via its foreign key (this is the "star join").
  3. WHERE r.Room_Type = 'Luxury' — filter on a dimension attribute.
  4. GROUP BY province, year, month — the dimension levels to report by.
  5. SUM(f.Revenue) — aggregates the measure.
  6. Pattern to remember: SELECT dimension attributes + aggregated measure FROM fact JOIN dimensions WHERE filters GROUP BY dimension attributes.

12. The Dimensional View — Data Cube (Hypercube)

  • Data can be viewed as a multidimensional cube with one axis per dimension.
  • Slide example: axes TIME (Jan, Feb, Mar), PRODUCT / room type (Luxury room, Budget room), PROVINCE (KZN, Gauteng). Each cell holds one value of the measure — e.g., one sales figure for Feb, Luxury room, Gauteng (R20m on the slide).
  • Note (slide): a data cube is a conceptual multidimensional representation, not necessarily physically stored as a cube (in ROLAP it is stored in relational tables).

Example (added)

With 3 months × 2 room types × 2 provinces the cube has 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 cells; each cell = one revenue figure. Adding a fourth dimension (e.g., promotion) makes a hyper-cube that can no longer be drawn simply, but works the same way.

13. Dimension Hierarchies — Roll-up and Drill-down

  • Hierarchies/categories within a dimension (e.g., Time: Year → Quarter → Month) are used to perform:
    • Roll-up: move up the hierarchy to a more summarised level (e.g., months → quarter).
    • Drill-down: move down the hierarchy to more detail (e.g., quarter → months).

Worked Example — Slide: Time hierarchy with revenue figures

  • The slide shows Qtr1 = R20m, Qtr2 (another figure), and the months under Qtr1 — Jan, Feb, Mar — with figures R5m, R10m, R5m.

Step-by-step reading (figures as they appear on the slide):

  1. Drill-down from Qtr1 (R20m) to months: Jan R5m + Feb R10m + Mar R5m = R20m — the detail adds up to the summary.
  2. Roll-up from the three months back to Qtr1 = R20m.
  3. Hierarchy used: Year → Quarter → Month (from the information package).
  4. Note: the slide's separate "hypercube" example shows R20m for Feb, Luxury room, Gauteng — a different illustration (one cell of a cube); the two R20m values are not the same quantity.
Operation Direction Detail level Example
Roll-up Up the hierarchy Less detail (summary) Branch → Province → Hotel line
Drill-down Down the hierarchy More detail Year → Quarter → Month

14. STARjoin, STARindex and Materialised Views

14.1 Performance Techniques (from the slides)

Technique Description
STARjoin Joins between a central fact table and multiple dimension tables.
STARindexes Specialised indexing techniques that can improve query performance on fact and dimension tables. Bitmap indexes are commonly used for low-cardinality dimension attributes in suitable warehouse workloads.
Materialised views Precomputed and stored query results that can improve analytical query performance; may need to be refreshed when the underlying data changes.

Examples (added for clarity)

  • Bitmap index: a column such as Room_Type (only a few distinct values: Luxury, Budget, …) can have one bit-vector per value marking which rows have that value — very fast to filter and combine (AND/OR) in analytical queries. Low cardinality = few distinct values.
  • Materialised view: store the result of "total revenue by province and month" once; queries read the stored summary instead of re-aggregating millions of fact rows. Trade-off: it must be refreshed after new data loads (storage and maintenance cost vs query speed).

15. SQL for Data Warehousing & BI

Task SQL statements (from the slide)
Creating the ROLAP structure CREATE SEQUENCE, CREATE TABLE, CREATE DIMENSION
Loading data into the ROLAP structure MERGE statement; UPDATE / INSERT statement
Generating reports SELECT … GROUP BY CUBE; SELECT … GROUP BY ROLLUP; SELECT … GROUP BY GROUPING SETS

15.1 CREATE DIMENSION (from the slide — Oracle-style syntax)

-- 1. First create the table
CREATE TABLE HOTEL ( ... );

-- 2. Then declare the dimension
CREATE DIMENSION HOTELS_DIM
  LEVEL Hotel     IS HOTEL.Hotel_code
  LEVEL Province  IS HOTEL.Province_code
  LEVEL Hotelline IS HOTEL.Hotelline_code
  HIERARCHY Hotel_Rollup
    ( Hotel CHILD OF Province CHILD OF Hotelline )
  ATTRIBUTE Hotel     DETERMINES (Hotel_name, Hotel_address, Hotel_city, ...)
  ATTRIBUTE Province  DETERMINES Province_name
  ATTRIBUTE Hotelline DETERMINES Hotelline_desc;

Step-by-step walkthrough:

  1. The table comes first: HOTEL holds columns Hotelline_code, Hotelline_desc, Province_code, Province_name, Hotel_key, Hotel_code, Hotel_name, Hotel_address, Hotel_city, Hotel_manager, Hotel_size (the slide's example hotel dimension).
  2. CREATE DIMENSION HOTELS_DIM declares metadata about the table — it does not create new data.
  3. LEVEL … IS … — defines the levels of the dimension (Hotel, Province, Hotelline) and the column that identifies each level.
  4. HIERARCHY Hotel_Rollup (Hotel CHILD OF Province CHILD OF Hotelline) — states the roll-up path: each hotel belongs to a province, each province to a hotel line (lowest → highest).
  5. ATTRIBUTE … DETERMINES … — a functional dependency among columns: the hotel code determines the hotel's name, address, city; the province code determines the province name; the hotel-line code determines its description.
  6. Purpose: this metadata lets the database understand the hierarchy — e.g., for query rewrite and materialised-view optimisation and OLAP roll-ups (purpose added).
  7. Note (added): CREATE DIMENSION is vendor-specific (Oracle), not standard SQL — in other systems the hierarchy is represented only through the table design and queries.

15.2 Loading — MERGE (added explanation)

MERGE INTO HOTEL h
USING staging_hotel s ON (h.Hotel_code = s.Hotel_code)
WHEN MATCHED     THEN UPDATE SET h.Hotel_name = s.Hotel_name, h.Hotel_city = s.Hotel_city
WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT (Hotel_code, Hotel_name, Hotel_city)
                      VALUES (s.Hotel_code, s.Hotel_name, s.Hotel_city);
  • MERGE = "update if the row exists, otherwise insert" in one statement — ideal for loading dimension tables from staging data (the Lecture 18 MERGE in Cypher was the same idea: match-or-create).

15.3 Reports — GROUP BY ROLLUP / CUBE / GROUPING SETS (added explanation; slide lists the names only)

Using the hotel star schema:

-- ROLLUP: subtotals going UP the hierarchy (right to left) + grand total
SELECT h.Province, t.Year, SUM(f.Revenue)
FROM ... GROUP BY ROLLUP (h.Province, t.Year);

-- CUBE: subtotals for EVERY combination of the listed columns
SELECT h.Province, t.Year, SUM(f.Revenue)
FROM ... GROUP BY CUBE (h.Province, t.Year);

-- GROUPING SETS: only the specific groupings you list
SELECT h.Province, t.Year, SUM(f.Revenue)
FROM ... GROUP BY GROUPING SETS ((h.Province), (t.Year));
Clause Groupings produced for (Province, Year)
ROLLUP (Province, Year) (Province, Year), (Province), () grand total
CUBE (Province, Year) (Province, Year), (Province), (Year), () grand total
GROUPING SETS ((Province), (Year)) (Province) and (Year) only
  • Link to concepts: ROLLUP = roll-up along a hierarchy; CUBE = all combinations — the full cube of aggregates.
  • Example output (added), ROLLUP on made-up data: rows for each (Gauteng, 2025), (Gauteng, 2026), a Gauteng subtotal row with Year = NULL, similarly for KZN, then a grand total row with both NULL.

16. Questions & Memo

Sections 2–4 — Business Environment, Operational vs Informational, BI

Q1: Distinguish operational from informational systems (purpose, users, classification, an example).

A1: Operational: "make the wheels turn" — record/process transactions as they happen; used by shop-floor workers; OLTP; e.g., POS, inventory, general ledger, claims. Informational: "watch the wheels turn" — support informed decisions; used by managers and analysts; OLAP; e.g., "show me the top-selling products", alerts when a branch sells below target.

Q2: What is business intelligence?

A2: A class of applications and technologies to gather, provide access to and analyse data about business operations, giving in-depth knowledge of factors such as customer/supplier behaviour and sales/production volumes, to help managers make better decisions.

Q3: Why is a data warehouse a viable solution for informational systems? (three reasons)

A3: It holds data from multiple applications; contains current and historical data; and the data is designed for analytical tasks (key measures and business dimensions).

Section 6 — Definition

Q4: Define a data warehouse and explain its four characteristics.

A4: A collection of data to support management decisions that is subject-oriented (organised by business subjects like sales), integrated (from different operational systems), non-volatile (read-oriented; changed only via controlled loading/correction), time-variant (time explicitly stored; analysis by day/week/month/quarter/year).

Section 7 & 8 — Architecture and Roles

Q5: List the main components of the data warehousing environment.

A5: Source data (internal/external); data staging (ETL/ELT); enterprise data warehouse; metadata; data marts; information delivery (data mining, OLAP, reports); interactive users; management & control; hardware/OS/DBMS; DB personnel.

Q6: Explain ETL and how ELT differs.

A6: ETL: Extract from sources, Transform (clean/integrate) in a staging area, Load into the warehouse. ELT: Extract, Load raw data first, then transform inside the analytical platform — common in modern cloud warehouses.

Q7: Name the four data-warehousing roles and what each does.

A7: Data transformation specialists (extraction, integration, transformation); data warehouse administrators (what data goes in, when, how); data modellers (relational and dimensional modelling); business analysts (design/create required reports).

Section 9–10 — Dimensional Modelling, Data Mart

Q8: Give the subject and dimensions for (a) a supermarket, (b) an airline, (c) a hotel chain.

A8: (a) Sales — time, branch, product, promotion. (b) Frequent flyers — time, customer, flight, fare class, airport. (c) Hotel occupancy — time, hotel branch, room type.

Q9: Is the time dimension compulsory in a dimensional model? Why is it so common?

A9: No — not every model needs it; but it is very common because historical, time-based analysis is important in many businesses.

Q10 (Analysis): For "How much revenue did luxury rooms generate month by month in Gauteng by hotel branch?" identify the measure and the dimensions involved.

A10: Measure: revenue. Dimensions: Room type (luxury), Time (month), Hotel/location (Gauteng province, by branch).

Q11: What is an information package and how is it used?

A11: A table created during requirements gathering, one per subject, listing dimensions with their hierarchies/categories and the facts (key measures); it is then converted into dimension tables and a fact table.

Q12: What is a data mart and why implement one?

A12: A focused subset of the warehouse for one department/subject. Reasons (see Section 10.1): focus on a department's needs, faster queries, quicker/cheaper to build, simpler for users, better access control.

Section 11 — Star Schema

Q13: Describe the star schema and what a fact table row contains.

A13: A central fact table linked by foreign keys to surrounding dimension tables (one-to-many). It provides ROLAP — a dimensional view using relational structures. A fact row represents a business event/observation at a defined granularity, with foreign keys to the dimensions and one or more measures.

Q14 (Design): From the information package (Time: Year/Quarter/Month; Hotel: Hotel line/Province/Branch; Room type: Room type/Size; facts: occupied rooms, vacant rooms, revenue), design the star schema.

A14: Dimension tables: TIME(Time_Key, Time_code, Year, Quarter, Month); HOTEL(Hotel_Key, Hotel_code, Hotel_Line, Province, Branch); RM_TYPE(Room_Key, Room_code, Room_Type, Size). Fact table: OCCUPANCY_FACTS(Time_Key, Hotel_Key, Room_Key, Occupied_rooms, Vacant_rooms, Revenue) with the three keys as foreign keys.

Q15 (Code): Write SQL for "total revenue from luxury rooms by province by month."

A15: See Section 11.2 — join OCCUPANCY_FACTS to HOTEL, RM_TYPE, TIME; WHERE Room_Type = 'Luxury'; GROUP BY Province, Year, Month; SUM(Revenue).

Section 12 & 13 — Cube, Hierarchies

Q16: What is a data cube? Must it be physically stored as a cube?

A16: A conceptual multidimensional representation — axes are dimensions, each cell a measure value (e.g., revenue for Feb, Luxury room, Gauteng). It is not necessarily physically stored as a cube (ROLAP uses relational tables).

Q17 (Tracing): Qtr1 revenue is R20m. Jan is R5m and Feb is R10m. What is Mar? Which operation moves from Qtr1 to the months, and which back?

A17: Mar = 20 − 5 − 10 = R5m. Drill-down goes Qtr1 → months; roll-up goes months → Qtr1.

Q18: Define roll-up and drill-down with a hierarchy example.

A18: Roll-up — move up a hierarchy to summarised data (Branch → Province → Hotel line). Drill-down — move down to more detail (Year → Quarter → Month).

Section 14 & 15 — Performance and SQL

Q19: Explain STARjoin, STARindex and materialised views.

A19: STARjoin: joins between the central fact table and multiple dimension tables. STARindex: specialised indexing (e.g., bitmap indexes for low-cardinality dimension attributes) to speed up queries. Materialised view: precomputed, stored query result that speeds up analytics but must be refreshed when underlying data changes.

Q20: What does CREATE DIMENSION define in the HOTELS_DIM example?

A20: Levels (Hotel, Province, Hotelline), the hierarchy Hotel CHILD OF Province CHILD OF Hotelline, and attribute dependencies (e.g., Hotel determines name/address/city; Province determines province name; Hotelline determines description).

Q21: List the SQL statements used to (a) create, (b) load and (c) report from a ROLAP structure.

A21: (a) CREATE SEQUENCE, CREATE TABLE, CREATE DIMENSION; (b) MERGE, UPDATE/INSERT; (c) SELECT … GROUP BY CUBE | ROLLUP | GROUPING SETS.

Q22 (Tracing): What groupings are produced by GROUP BY ROLLUP (Province, Year) and by GROUP BY CUBE (Province, Year)?

A22: ROLLUP: (Province, Year), (Province), grand total. CUBE: (Province, Year), (Province), (Year), grand total.

Q23 (Scenario): A supermarket chain's head office runs heavy sales analyses on the same database its tills use, and the tills slow down. Recommend a solution and explain.

A23: Build a data warehouse (or data mart) fed by ETL/ELT from the POS and inventory systems: it holds integrated, historical, analysis-oriented data separate from the OLTP system, organised as a star schema (sales fact + time/branch/product/promotion dimensions), so managers' OLAP queries no longer compete with till transactions.
